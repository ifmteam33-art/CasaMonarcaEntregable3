Risk Analysis
What Could Go Wrong?

The main risks Arauco faces beyond day-to-day economic fluctuations.

High

Legal Uncertainty

Mexico changed how judges are elected, weakening legal protections for businesses. Moody's and Fitch warned this could damage foreign investment. For Arauco: harder to enforce contracts and protect their $290M USD investment.

High

Energy Problems

Mexico's electricity grid (CFE) has not expanded fast enough for new industrial demand. Arauco's plants require significant electricity. Outages could halt production and raise costs significantly.

Medium-High

Security & Crime

Organized crime controls some transport routes. Companies allocate 2–10% of their budgets to security. Arauco's weekly shipments from Durango to North American plants face supply chain risk.

Critical Global Risk

The 2026 Iran War

In February 2026, military operations blocked 20% of global oil supply. Oil jumped from $73 to over $110 per barrel. For Arauco: higher energy costs, more expensive chemical inputs, and weaker U.S. housing demand.

Critical

USMCA Trade Review

The North American trade agreement goes under formal review in July 2026. Arauco ships products between Mexico and the U.S. every week under this deal. Rule changes could severely disrupt their supply chain.

Opportunity

Nearshoring & Sustainability

Arauco holds FSC sustainability certification — increasingly required by U.S. and European retailers. Mexico's growing AI data center build-out creates new demand for Arauco's industrial panels.

Hedging Strategy
What Does Arauco Need to Do?

Based on the macroeconomic analysis and structural risks identified, this section outlines the key conclusions and the risk management framework Arauco must build before designing any specific hedging instrument.

Key Conclusions from the Analysis
Three Critical Findings
01

The Rate Gap is Shrinking

The interest rate differential — currently ~300 bps in Mexico's favor — is the dominant driver of USD/MXN in the short to medium term. As Banxico ends its easing cycle at 6.50% while the Fed holds (with potential hike risk), this differential is compressing. Every 25 bps of convergence weakens the carry-trade incentive that has anchored peso strength since 2023, gradually increasing the MXN cost of Arauco's USD-indexed chemical inputs.

Source: Rio Times Online, April 2026 · Deliverable 2, Conclusion Section

02

Domestic Demand is Under Pressure

Mexico's Q1 2026 GDP contraction of -0.8% — the steepest since Q4 2024 — confirms that domestic panel demand is under pressure from multiple angles: 35 consecutive months of falling formal manufacturing employment, declining consumer purchasing power from lower remittances (-7.5% YoY), and 17 consecutive months of investment contraction.

Source: Mexico Business News, May 2026 · Deliverable 2, Conclusion Section

03

The Nearshoring Thesis Holds — But Selectively

The nearshoring investment thesis underpinning Arauco's USD 290M combined investment in Durango and Zitácuaro remains structurally intact, but is entering a more selective, risk-adjusted phase. The July 2026 USMCA review is the single most consequential near-term event: a positive outcome extends the investment super-cycle; a breakdown triggers both a sharp peso sell-off and a disruption of intra-company supply flows.

Source: CSIS, April 2026 · Deliverable 2, Conclusion Section

Structural Risk Conclusions
Three Additional Structural Findings

Fiscal Constraints Limit Government Support

Mexico's fiscal trajectory — deficit at 4.1% of GDP, record public debt at 52.3% of GDP, and PEMEX absorbing MXN 263.5B in obligations — constrains the government's capacity to stimulate the economy or invest in productive infrastructure. This limits the pipeline of construction projects that generate demand for Arauco's panels.

Source: Americas Quarterly, December 2025 · Deliverable 2, Section III Group A

Institutional Risks are Structural, Not Transient

Mexico's judicial reform has weakened contract enforcement protections, energy re-nationalization has created grid bottlenecks in the northern manufacturing belt where Arauco operates, and organized crime adds 2–10% in security costs to manufacturing operations. These are not temporary — they must be priced into Arauco's long-term return expectations.

Source: Mexico Affairs, January 2026 · Mexecution, 2026 · Deliverable 2, Section III Group B

The Iran War Creates a Multi-Channel Shock

The 2026 Iran war affects Arauco through three simultaneous channels: higher energy and logistics costs at its Durango and Zitácuaro plants, higher methanol-derived resin input prices regardless of the exchange rate, and weaker North American construction demand as U.S. consumer spending power is eroded by elevated gasoline prices.

Source: LSE Middle East Centre, April 2026 · CBS News, May 2026 · Deliverable 2, Section III Group C

Risk Management Framework
What Arauco Needs Before Hedging Anything

"Before the firm hedges anything, it must know exactly what risks it has, how large they are, and which ones matter most." — Deliverable 2 Conclusion

Step 1 — Identify & Quantify Each Exposure

Map every FX-sensitive cash flow precisely — chemical inputs (resins, formalin), USD-denominated debt (SOFR-linked), and intra-company cross-border shipments — and quantify each in both MXN and USD terms.

Step 2 — Assess Natural Hedges First

Before using derivatives, evaluate the natural hedges already embedded in Arauco's operations — primarily, the ability to offset USD input costs with USD revenues from its 8 North American plants. This reduces the net exposure that actually needs to be hedged.

Step 3 — Select Proportionate Instruments

Only after quantifying net exposure should Arauco select derivative instruments — forwards, options, or interest rate swaps — that are proportionate, cost-effective, and aligned with its risk appetite and budget planning requirements.

Bottom line: Arauco's Mexican operations are well-positioned strategically as the primary wood panel producer in Mexico, with FSC-certified products and exposure to the most dynamic demand driver in the region (nearshoring). However, the convergence of a compressing rate differential, a contracting Mexican economy, a global energy shock, and institutional uncertainty creates a set of interacting risks that cannot be managed through a single hedging instrument or a single policy. A comprehensive, instrument-specific hedging strategy is required — and it begins with the diagnostic work presented in this report. Source: Deliverable 2 — Conclusion, Team 3
